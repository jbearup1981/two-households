Payroll & taxes — the part that's been missing

The business became an S-corp on January 1, which means Jason has to be paid a real W-2 salary. That hasn't happened yet this year. Here's the fix, and it's a normal one — the IRS looks at the yearly total, not the timing.

Filing jointly saves us about $8,450 versus filing separately. The whole bill gets withheld from those eight paychecks — no surprise in April, no quarterly payments to remember. Withholding counts as if it were paid evenly all year, which is exactly why doing this now works.

There is still tax owed on the business profit above the salary — that's the $16,968 figure, and it's the number those eight paychecks are sized to cover. Separately, the $500/month going to the IRS is for prior years and continues untouched. That older balance is roughly $5,000–6,000 owed now, plus another $5,000–8,000 expected when the 2025 return is finished — carried here at $20,000 to be safe. Nailing that number down is worth doing; it decides whether the house proceeds clear it outright.

A full year of tax gets paid in three and a half months — $7,252 a month where the normal rate is $2,115. Nothing is wrong here; it's the cost of catching payroll up in one run. It does mean the debt sits at minimum payments until January, and the $8,520 showing up in Jason's account is not spare money — it goes back to the business to cover the gap. The real paydown starts in the new year.

And the gap has a cover. Jason is selling the John Deere and some other things around the house — call it $3,000 to $4,000. That lands almost exactly on the $3,939 shortfall, which is the difference between coasting through the fall and reaching for a credit card again. It's the one piece of this that should happen in the next few weeks.
