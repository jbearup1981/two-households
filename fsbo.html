Selling It Ourselves

A realtor's commission on this house would cost more than the entire equity we expect to walk away with. That is the whole reason to consider doing it ourselves.

The math

We expect to clear somewhere around $20,000 at close. Here is what a commission takes out of that.

Read the top line again: a full commission can cost more than everything we walk away with. That is not a criticism of realtors — it is what a percentage fee does to a sale with thin equity. On a house with $150,000 of equity the same 6% would be an annoyance. On ours it is the whole thing.

Across likely sale prices, full 6% commission

$16,500 – $25,500

At $275,000 it is $16,500. At $425,000 it is $25,500. In every case it is roughly equal to or greater than the equity — which means the money that would pay off the back taxes and the student loans is the same money that would pay the commission.

And even in the likely case — where the buyer brings an agent and we pay that side 2.5% — we still keep about $9,300 after every cost above.

The flat-fee MLS listing is not optional. Almost every buyer finds houses through Zillow, Realtor.com and Redfin, and those sites pull from the MLS. A house that is not on the MLS is invisible to most of the market. For $300–500 a flat-fee broker puts it there and we handle everything else ourselves — that single line item is most of what a listing agent was doing.

3

The steps, in order

01Price it from evidence, not hope Overpricing is the single most common way this fails. A house that sits for three weeks gets treated as damaged goods and sells for less than if it had been priced right on day one. Pay for an appraisal or a market analysis and then believe the number.
02Get the pre-listing inspection Find the problems before a buyer's inspector does. Anything discovered after an accepted offer becomes a price renegotiation from a weak position. Anything fixed beforehand is just a fixed thing.
03Finish the repairs — including the egress window That window makes it a legal four-bedroom, which moves both the price and the pool of buyers far more than the $900 it costs. It is the highest-return item on the punch list.
04Professional photos, after the repairs Not phone photos. The listing photos are the entire first impression for nearly every buyer, and they are why someone books a showing.
05Decide the buyer-agent offer before listing Since the 2024 rule changes, buyer agents negotiate their fee with their own buyers rather than taking a posted split. Decide up front whether to offer 2–2.5%, and be consistent. Offering something keeps agents willing to show it; offering nothing narrows the buyer pool but keeps every dollar.
06Line up the title company early In Michigan the title company runs the closing. Pick one before the first offer so there is no scramble, and ask what they need from a for-sale-by-owner seller.
07Have an attorney review the purchase agreement This is the one place not to save money. A few hundred dollars against a three-hundred-thousand-dollar contract, on the document that decides what happens if something goes wrong. Everything else here is worth doing yourself; this is not.
08Screen for a mortgage pre-approval before accepting Not a pre-qualification — a pre-approval from an actual lender. A buyer who cannot close wastes weeks and the house goes back on the market looking stale.
4

What Michigan requires

Seller's Disclosure StatementRequired by law on residential sales. Fill it out honestly and completely — this is the document that comes back at you later if something was hidden.
Lead-based paint disclosureFederal requirement if the house was built before 1978.
No attorney required at closingThe title company handles it. This is why for-sale-by-owner is more workable in Michigan than in states that require an attorney at the table.

Nothing here is legal advice — it is the practical shape of the thing. The disclosure statement and the purchase agreement are the two documents where getting it wrong is expensive.

The timeline

Working backward from being in the new place before Thanksgiving.

  • Oct 16Repairs finished, egress window in
  • Oct 23Photos taken, price set from the appraisal
  • Oct 25MLS listing goes live
  • Nov 2Showings and offers
  • Nov 6Accepted offer
  • Nov 10Buyer's inspection and appraisal
  • Nov 15Title work and financing
  • Nov 20Close

That is a tight but realistic schedule only if the repairs finish on time. Every week the punch list slips pushes the close past Thanksgiving and into the holidays, which is the worst stretch of the year to have a house on the market.

6

Where this can go wrong

Worth naming honestly, because the savings are real but so are these.

OverpricingThe most common failure by a wide margin. Without an agent nobody is professionally obliged to tell you the number is too high. The appraisal is the antidote — and only if you believe it.
Some agents steer around usLess true since the 2024 rule changes, but a buyer's agent working for free has little reason to bring anyone. Offering 2–2.5% mostly solves it.
TimeShowings, calls, scheduling — during the same weeks as first payroll, the 2025 return and a move. This is the cost people underestimate.
Negotiating without a bufferAn agent absorbs the awkward parts. Doing it yourself means hearing criticism of your house directly and staying commercial about it.

The fallback, decided in advance

Three weeks, then reassess

If the listing is live and there is no serious interest after three weeks, that is the signal — either the price is wrong or the approach is. Deciding that now, while nothing is at stake, is much easier than deciding it in November with the move looming.
